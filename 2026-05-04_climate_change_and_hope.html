Climate
Change

Where these gases come from, how we measure them, and what the evidence tells us.

Key idea

Natural vs. Human-caused

The greenhouse effect is natural — it has always kept Earth warm enough for life. The problem today is that human activity is making it stronger.

🌿
Natural greenhouse effect
Always existed. Keeps Earth at +15°C. Driven by natural processes.
🏭
Anthropogenic effect
Caused by humans. Adding extra greenhouse gases. Making the planet warmer than it should be.
📖 Key word

Anthropogenic (an-thro-po-JEN-ik) means "caused by humans." When scientists talk about anthropogenic climate change, they mean warming caused by human activities — not natural changes.

Sources of Greenhouse Gases

Where does CO₂ come from?

CO₂ is released when we burn things that contain carbon.

  • 🔥 Burning fossil fuels — coal, oil, natural gas — about 75% of all human CO₂
  • 🌳 Deforestation — trees store carbon; when cut or burned, that carbon releases as CO₂
  • 🏗️ Making cement and steel — large CO₂ releases during manufacturing
  • 🚗 Transport — cars, trucks, planes burning petrol and diesel
🌿 Natural sources too

Animals breathing, volcanoes, and decomposing plants all release CO₂ naturally. But these are balanced by natural carbon sinks (forests, oceans) that absorb it. The problem is the extra CO₂ humans add — natural sinks cannot absorb it all.

carbon sink (KAR-bun sink) — a natural reservoir that absorbs CO₂ from the atmosphere; forests and oceans are the main carbon sinks
Sources of Greenhouse Gases

Where does methane come from?

Methane (CH₄) traps about 80 times more heat per molecule than CO₂ over a 20-year period — meaning each molecule of methane causes much more warming than a molecule of CO₂. However, methane breaks down in about 12 years, unlike CO₂ which persists for centuries.

  • 🐄 Livestock — microbes in cattle stomachs ferment food and release CH₄ (enteric fermentation)
  • 🌾 Rice fields — anaerobic microbes in flooded, oxygen-poor soils produce CH₄
  • 🗑️ Landfills — microbes decomposing organic waste underground release CH₄
  • 🌿 Wetlands — the largest natural source; microbial activity in waterlogged soils
  • 🛢️ Oil and gas operations — CH₄ leaks directly from wells and pipelines
💡 Why does this matter?

Because methane breaks down in ~12 years, cutting methane emissions today is one of the fastest ways to slow near-term warming — faster than cutting CO₂.

Carbon Sinks

Forests and oceans absorb CO₂

Not all the CO₂ humans release stays in the atmosphere. Natural carbon sinks absorb some of it — but they have limits.

🌳
Forests
Trees absorb CO₂ through photosynthesis and store carbon in their wood and roots. 50% of biomass by weight is carbon. Deforestation releases this stored carbon.
🌊
Oceans (the hydrosphere)
The hydrosphere — all water on Earth — absorbs about 25–30% of all human CO₂ emissions. Oceans also absorb heat, acting as a giant buffer that slows surface warming.
⚠️ Ocean acidification — the chemistry

When CO₂ dissolves in seawater, it reacts with water (H₂O) to form carbonic acid (H₂CO₃), which then breaks apart into bicarbonate and a proton — making the water more acidic:

\( {CO}_2 + {H}_2{O} > {HCO}_3^- + {H}^+ \)

More H⁺ ions = more acidic seawater. This threatens coral reefs and shellfish that need carbonate ions to build their shells — rising CO₂ makes those carbonate ions less available.

hydrosphere (HY-dro-sfeer) — all water on Earth: oceans, lakes, rivers, ice, and water vapour  |  ocean acidification — the process by which oceans become more acidic as they absorb CO₂
Measuring Gases

How do we measure gases?

Scientists use different units to describe how much of a gas exists. Understanding these units helps you read and compare data.

👆 Tap each unit to see what it means

⚖️
ppm — parts per million
How many molecules of a gas exist in every million air molecules. CO₂ is currently ~422 ppm. Useful for gases present in small but significant amounts.
🔬
ppb — parts per billion
How many molecules in every billion air molecules. Used for trace gases like methane (~1,900 ppb = 1.9 ppm) and nitrous oxide (~330 ppb). 1 ppm = 1,000 ppb.
📊
% — percentage (mass fraction)
A mass fraction tells you what proportion of a mixture's total mass is made up of one particular component. For the atmosphere: Nitrogen = 78%, Oxygen = 21%, Argon = 0.93% — these are measured by mass. CO₂ = only about 0.042% by mass. This seems tiny, but mass fraction alone does not tell you how powerful a gas is — which is why we also need ppm and GWP.
🌍
GtC — gigatons of carbon
Used to measure how much carbon is released or stored globally. "Giga" means one billion — so 1 gigaton = 1,000,000,000 tonnes (that is 1 followed by 9 zeros). All human activities combined currently release about 10 GtC per year. Important: this is carbon (C) only. CO₂ is heavier than C alone because each carbon atom carries two oxygen atoms. To convert: 1 GtC = 3.67 GtCO₂. So 10 GtC of carbon emissions = about 37 GtCO₂. Always check which unit a source is using.
Comparing Greenhouse Gases

Not all gases are equal

To compare greenhouse gases, scientists use Global Warming Potential (GWP) — how much warming one unit of a gas causes compared to CO₂.

📖 What does "reference = 1" mean?

In science, a reference is a standard we use to compare everything else against. Here, CO₂ is given a value of 1. A gas with a value of 80× is 80 times more warming than the same mass of CO₂.

Methane — it is 80× more powerful over 20 years and breaks down in ~12 years, so cuts have rapid near-term impact. But CO₂ is also essential long-term because it persists for centuries. Ideally: cut both, but methane gives the fastest results.
💡 Scientific thinking

We cannot judge a gas by one number alone. We must consider: how much of it exists, how long it lasts, and how powerful it is. Good science always looks at the full picture.

Measuring CO₂

The Keeling Curve

Since 1958, scientists have measured CO₂ in the atmosphere every day at Mauna Loa Observatory in Hawaii. The result is one of the most important graphs in science.

📊 What the graph shows

CO₂ has risen from 313 ppm in 1958 to 422 ppm in 2024 — a 35% increase.

Northern Hemisphere plants absorb CO₂ in spring and summer (photosynthesis), then release it in autumn and winter when they decay. More land = more plants = this pattern dominates globally.
observatory (ob-ZER-va-tor-ee) — a place where scientists watch and measure things in the sky or atmosphere
800,000 Years of Evidence

What ancient ice tells us

We can only directly measure CO₂ since 1958. But ice cores from Antarctica extend the record back 800,000 years.

🔬 How it works

Each year, snow falls and compresses into ice, trapping tiny air bubbles. Scientists drill down approximately 3 km to reach ice that is 800,000 years old. The bubbles contain real samples of ancient atmosphere.

Ancient temperatures are measured by analysing oxygen isotopes in the ice itself — different isotopes of oxygen are incorporated into ice in different proportions depending on the temperature at the time it formed, giving scientists a record of past temperatures.

  • CO₂ and temperature always rose and fell together over 800,000 years
  • CO₂ naturally ranged from 180–280 ppm — never above 300 ppm
  • Today: 422 ppm — higher than at any point in 800,000 years
  • The sharp jump on the right of the graph = the Industrial Revolution
🕳️ Fun fact — the deepest hole ever drilled

The Kola Superdeep Borehole in Russia was drilled to 12,262 metres — the deepest artificial point on Earth. At that depth, temperatures reached 180°C — far hotter than expected and so hot that drilling had to stop. Ice cores go only ~3 km down, but through ice rather than rock — which is much easier to drill through!

📊 See the 800,000-year graph — NASA →
ice core — a tube of ancient ice drilled from a glacier; air bubbles inside contain old atmosphere  |  Industrial Revolution (in-DUS-tree-ul) — ~1750 onward; when large-scale burning of coal began
Indicators of Climate Change

Signs that climate is changing

Scientists look for indicators — measurable signs that the climate is changing. These come from many different sources.

🧊Melting ice and rising sea levels ▼
Glaciers and polar ice sheets are shrinking. As ice melts, sea levels rise — threatening coastal cities and islands. Arctic sea ice is also thinning, reducing habitat for polar bears and other cold-adapted species.

There is also a dangerous feedback loop connected to a principle you are learning in physics: white surfaces reflect all wavelengths of light (high albedo), while dark surfaces absorb all wavelengths (low albedo). It is the absorption of light that generates heat. Ice is white and highly reflective — it bounces sunlight back to space. Dark ocean water absorbs sunlight and converts it to heat. So when ice melts and exposes dark ocean, more solar energy is absorbed, causing more warming, melting more ice. Less ice → darker surface → more heat absorbed → even less ice.
🌡️Heatwaves and extreme weather ▼
Heatwaves are becoming longer and more intense. Storms are becoming stronger. Droughts and floods are more frequent. In Canada, hotter and drier summers have caused larger forest fires.
🐠Effects on living things ▼
Coral reefs are bleaching and dying as oceans warm and acidify. Migratory birds are arriving earlier. Some species are shifting toward the poles. Crop harvests are disrupted by changing rainfall patterns.
🌾Food and water security ▼
Changing rainfall patterns affect when crops can be planted. Glaciers that supply freshwater to millions of people are shrinking. In some regions, growing seasons are getting longer; in others, farmland is becoming desert.
indicator (IN-di-kay-tor) — a measurable sign that something is changing  |  albedo (al-BEE-doh) — how reflective a surface is; ice has high albedo (reflects sunlight); dark ocean has low albedo (absorbs heat)

Individual Action

What is a carbon footprint?

Your carbon footprint is the total amount of greenhouse gases produced by your actions — travelling, eating, using electricity, and buying things.

✈️Travel ▼
Flying produces large amounts of CO₂ per passenger. Car travel produces less. Walking, cycling, and public transport produce least. The further you travel, the larger the footprint.
🍖Food ▼
Beef and lamb have the highest footprint (methane from livestock + land use). Plant-based foods have much lower footprints. Eating locally grown food reduces transport emissions.
💡Energy at home ▼
Heating homes with gas or oil releases CO₂. Electricity from coal is high-footprint; from solar or wind is very low. LED bulbs, turning off lights, and not over-heating rooms all reduce emissions.
🌍 Small actions add up

Individual actions matter — especially when millions of people make the same choices. Walking or cycling instead of driving, reducing meat consumption, and using less electricity all reduce your carbon footprint.

carbon footprint (KAR-bun FOOT-print) — the total greenhouse gases produced by a person's activities, measured in units of CO₂
The Challenge

Can we stop?

We cannot stop using all fossil fuels immediately — billions of people depend on them. But we can reduce in stages:

  • Near term: Stop CO₂ from rising — reach the peak and start going down
  • Medium term: Replace fossil fuels with clean energy — solar, wind, electric vehicles
  • Long term: Reach net zero — removing as much CO₂ as we add
🇪🇺 EU: net zero by 2050
🇨🇳 China: 2060
🇮🇳 India: 2070
⚠️ Some warming is already locked in

Even if we reached net zero today, temperatures would still rise a little for 2–3 more decades — the ocean has stored heat that will slowly release. This is why acting sooner matters so much.

net zero (net ZEE-roh) — when CO₂ released equals CO₂ removed; the total in the atmosphere stops rising  |  commitment (kuh-MIT-ment) — a formal promise to do something

Case Study

Pakistan's solar revolution

When electricity prices rose 155% in three years, ordinary Pakistanis started buying cheap solar panels themselves. No government program. A people-led revolution.

Solar share of Pakistan's electricity — 2021 to 2025
🔋
Energy independence
Less reliance on imported fossil fuels — more stability, less exposure to price spikes

Satellite Technology

MethaneSAT — finding leaks from space

To cut methane emissions, you first have to find them — surprisingly hard from the ground.

🔭 How it worked

MethaneSAT is a satellite that orbits Earth 15 times per day (launched March 2024). It detects methane by measuring which wavelengths of sunlight are "missing" after passing through the atmosphere. It maps leaks across many countries — including leaks too small or spread out to detect any other way.

🔍 A striking finding

Texas and New Mexico (states in the USA) share the same oil fields and companies — yet Texas had twice the methane leaks. The difference? New Mexico introduced stricter regulations in 2021. Independent data creates accountability.

🛰️ methanesat.org →
satellite (SAT-uh-lyt) — a machine in orbit around Earth gathering information  |  accountable (uh-KOWN-tuh-bul) — responsible for actions; unable to hide what you are doing
