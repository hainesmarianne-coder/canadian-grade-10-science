Why is a year 365 days long?

Earth travels all the way around the Sun once every 365.25 days. We call this one solar year.

Because 0.25 of a day adds up over time, every calendar needs a way to stay in sync with the seasons. In the Gregorian calendar (used internationally), the year begins on 1 January, and a leap day is added on February 29 every 4 years. In the Solar Hijri calendar, the year begins on Nowruz — 1 Hamal (around 20–21 March), and leap years follow a 33-year cycle with a day added to the last month. In Dari, a leap year is called Kabise. Both solutions work for the same astronomical reason — the orbit takes 365.25 days.

📅 Two calendars, same Sun

Today in the Gregorian calendar is 29 April 2026. In the Solar Hijri calendar, today is 9 Sawr (Taurus) 1405. The new Solar Hijri year 1405 began on Nowruz — 20 March 2026. The Solar Hijri calendar is the official civil calendar of Iran and Afghanistan, and Nowruz is celebrated by an estimated 300 million people across a wide region: including Azerbaijan, India, Iraq, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Pakistan, Tajikistan, Turkey, Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan, and Iranian diaspora communities around the world.

📏 The Sun is 150 million km from Earth
⚡ Earth travels around the Sun at 107,000 km/h
💡 Light takes 8.3 minutes to travel from the Sun's surface to Earth
orbit (OR-bit) — the curved path one object takes around another in space  |  solar year (SOH-lar year) — the time it takes Earth to go all the way around the Sun once  |  Kabise (kah-BEE-seh) — the Dari word for a leap year; a year with an extra day added to keep the calendar in sync with the seasons

Why is a day 24 hours?

A day is the time for Earth to complete one full spin on its axis. Earth spins from west to east — this is why the Sun rises in the east and sets in the west. If you could look down at the North Pole from space, you would see Earth spinning anti-clockwise (the opposite direction to a clock).

🤔 Interesting point

One true full spin actually takes 23 hours and 56 minutes. But we use 24 hours because Earth is also moving along its orbit at the same time. By the time Earth has finished spinning, it has moved a little bit around the Sun — so it needs to spin just a tiny bit more to bring the Sun back to the same place in the sky.

axis (AK-sis) — an imaginary line through the middle of Earth from the North Pole to the South Pole, around which Earth spins  |  anti-clockwise (an-tee-KLOK-wyz) — turning in the opposite direction to the hands of a clock

Why do seasons change?

🌍 Why seasons happen

Seasons happen because Earth is tilted on its axis by 23.5°. This tilt stays pointing the same direction as Earth moves around the Sun — so sometimes the Northern Hemisphere leans toward the Sun (summer), and sometimes away from it (winter).

▶️ Watch: Why Do We Have Seasons? (YouTube · 3 min)
hemisphere (HEM-is-feer) — half of the Earth; the Northern Hemisphere is the top half, the Southern Hemisphere is the bottom half  |  tilted (TIL-ted) — leaning to one side, not perfectly straight up

The tilt changes two things at the same time. Tap each one to learn more:

🌞June, July, August — Summer in the North ▼
The north pole tilts toward the Sun. Sunlight hits the ground more directly (imagine shining a torch straight down — it makes a small, bright spot). Days are longer. In Kabul, temperatures can go above 35–45°C.
🍂September, October, November — Autumn in the North ▼
The tilt is moving away from the Sun. Days are getting shorter and temperatures are falling.
❄️December, January, February — Winter in the North ▼
The north pole tilts away from the Sun. Sunlight hits the ground at a low angle and spreads over a large area (like shining the torch at a slope — the light spreads out and is weaker). Days are shorter. Kabul can drop to −15°C.
🌱March, April, May — Spring in the North ▼
The tilt is moving back toward the Sun. Days are getting longer and warmer. In Afghanistan — snowmelt feeds the rivers and the growing season begins. Nowruz (1 Hamal) falls on the spring equinox, at the very start of this season, around 20–21 March.
🔄Opposite hemispheres, opposite seasons ▼
When Kabul has summer, Melbourne in Australia has winter — at exactly the same moment. The same tilt causes both. Nothing is different about the Sun; only your position relative to it changes.
equinox (EE-kwi-noks) — a day when every place on Earth gets ~12 hours of daylight; in the Solar Hijri calendar: 1 Hamal (spring) and 1 Mizan (autumn)  |  solstice (SOL-stis) — the day with the most (summer, mid-Saratan) or least (winter, mid-Jaddi) daylight of the year

Why does the Moon seem to change shape?

The Moon does not make its own light. We only see the part of the Moon that the Sun is shining on. As the Moon travels around Earth over about 29.5 days, we see more or less of its lit side. The shape does not really change — only the part we can see changes.

🌑
New Moon — tap to learn
The Moon is between Earth and the Sun. We see only its dark side — it looks invisible in the sky.
🌓
First Quarter — tap to learn
We can see the right half of the Moon lit up. It has completed one quarter of its journey around Earth.
🌕
Full Moon — tap to learn
Earth is between the Moon and the Sun — so the Sun's light shines past Earth and lights up the whole face of the Moon that we can see. You might think: "Doesn't Earth's shadow fall on the Moon then?" Usually no — the Moon's orbit is slightly tilted, so it passes above or below Earth's shadow most of the time. When it does pass through the shadow, that is a special event called a lunar eclipse, which only happens a few times a year.
🌗
Third Quarter — tap to learn
We can see the left half lit. Almost back to New Moon again.

How do tides work?

A tide is the slow rise and fall of the ocean's water level. If you stand on a beach and watch for several hours, the water comes in and covers the sand (high tide), then goes out and uncovers it again (low tide). This happens roughly twice a day.

Tides are caused by the Moon's gravity pulling on Earth's water. The pull is stronger on the side of Earth closest to the Moon, which creates a bulge of water there.

There is also a bulge on the opposite side of Earth. Why? Because the Moon's gravity pulls the whole Earth slightly toward it — the water on the far side gets "left behind," forming a second bulge. The result: as Earth rotates, each coast passes through these two bulges roughly twice each day, giving two high tides and two low tides.

▶️ Watch: How Do Tides Work? (YouTube)
tide (tyd) — the regular rise and fall of the ocean caused by the Moon's gravity  |  gravity (GRAV-i-tee) — an invisible force that pulls objects toward each other; the bigger the object, the stronger its gravity
🐌

Earth is slowly slowing down!

Every day, the ocean water moves back and forth with the tides. As it does, it rubs (creates friction) against the seafloor and coastlines. This friction acts like a very, very gentle brake on Earth's spin — so slowly that a single human lifetime makes no measurable difference, but over billions of years it adds up.

🌍 1.4 billion years ago: an 18-hour day
📅 620 million years ago: a 22-hour day
⏰ Today: 24 hours
🔭 ~3 billion years in the future: ~47-hour day
🌙 Where does the lost energy go?

When Earth loses spin energy to friction, that energy does not simply disappear — it is transferred to the Moon through gravity, pushing the Moon into a higher orbit. A higher orbit means a larger circle, further away from Earth. The Moon is moving away from Earth at about 3.8 cm per year. We know this very precisely because Apollo astronauts left laser reflectors on the Moon's surface, and scientists on Earth bounce laser beams off them and time the reflection.

friction (FRIK-shun) — a force created when two surfaces rub against each other, which slows things down and creates heat  |  higher orbit (HY-er OR-bit) — a path around Earth that is wider and further away; objects in higher orbits travel more slowly and take longer to go around

Artemis II — It just happened!

On April 1, 2026 — just 4 weeks ago — NASA sent four people to fly around the Moon and come back. It was the first time humans had travelled that far from Earth in more than 50 years!

  • Crew: Reid Wiseman, Victor Glover, Christina Koch (USA) + Jeremy Hansen (Canada 🇨🇦)
  • Farthest point: ~407,000 km from Earth — further than any human has ever been, breaking the record set in 1970
  • They photographed the far side of the Moon, ancient craters, and a rare solar eclipse seen from space
  • They witnessed an Earthrise — the moment when Earth appeared to rise above the edge of the Moon as they flew around it. From the Moon, Earth looks like a beautiful blue-and-white marble floating in the black sky of space. It is one of the most beautiful and moving sights any human has ever seen.
  • Splashed down April 10 · next mission (Artemis III) aims for a Moon landing in 2028

It's all connected

All of these things — days, years, seasons, and tides — are set by just a few numbers about Earth and the Moon. If any of these numbers were different, life on Earth would be very different:

🌍
Earth's spin speed
~1,670 km/h at the equator → sets how long a day is
📐
Earth's tilt
23.5° → creates the seasons
🌞
Time to orbit the Sun
~365.25 days → sets how long a year is
🌕
Moon's distance
~384,400 km → creates the tides
💡 A surprising fact

Earth's 23.5° tilt is kept stable by the Moon's gravity. Without the Moon, Earth's tilt could change wildly over thousands of years, causing extreme climate changes. We owe our stable, life-friendly climate partly to our large, nearby Moon!

Weather

The Greenhouse Effect

The greenhouse effect is a natural process that warms Earth's surface. Without it, Earth would be a frozen world with no life. With it, life is possible.

Without greenhouse gases
−18°C
🧊 Too cold for life
With natural greenhouse gases
+15°C
🌺 Life can exist!

That 33°C difference comes entirely from natural greenhouse gases. The problem today is that human activity is adding extra greenhouse gases on top.

greenhouse effect (GREEN-hows ef-EKT) — a natural process where certain gases in the atmosphere trap the Sun's heat and keep Earth warm  |  atmosphere (AT-mos-feer) — the layer of gases (air) that surrounds Earth

How does it work? 4 steps

👆 Tap each step to open it

📺 Tip for beginners: Organisations like NASA, NOAA and the BBC make short, simple science videos in easy English. When a science topic is new to you, these are excellent places to start — the science is accurate, and the language is clear. Don't be afraid to search for them!

▶️ Watch: The Greenhouse Effect Explained (YouTube)
☀️Step 1 — Sunlight arrives ▼
The Sun sends out light and energy. About 30% of this sunlight bounces back into space off clouds and ice. The other 70% passes through the atmosphere and is absorbed by the ocean, land, and ground.
🔥Step 2 — The surface gets warm ▼
When sunlight is absorbed by the ground or ocean, the surface heats up — just like how a dark road gets very hot on a sunny day. The surface then releases this energy back upward, but now as a different kind of radiation called infrared radiation.
🌡️Step 3 — Infrared radiation travels upward ▼

The warm surface releases infrared radiation upward into the atmosphere. Infrared radiation has a longer wavelength than the visible light that came from the Sun — it is invisible to our eyes.

Infrared radiation is not technically "heat" itself, but it creates heat when it is absorbed. When infrared waves hit a molecule, they cause that molecule to vibrate. This vibration is what we feel as warmth. This is the same reason your skin feels warm when you sit near a fire — infrared radiation from the fire is absorbed by your skin and causes its molecules to vibrate.

🪤Step 4 — Greenhouse gases trap it ▼
Greenhouse gas molecules in the atmosphere absorb this infrared radiation and send some of it back down toward Earth. This is like a blanket around Earth that keeps warmth in. Adding more greenhouse gases makes the blanket thicker, trapping more heat.
infrared radiation (IN-fra-red ray-dee-AY-shun) — a type of energy with a longer wavelength than visible light; invisible to our eyes but felt as warmth when absorbed  |  wavelength (WAYV-length) — the size of a wave of light or energy; different wavelengths of light look like different colours, or may be invisible like infrared

Which gases are greenhouse gases?

👆 Tap each gas to learn more!

💨
Carbon dioxide (CO₂)
The most important long-lived greenhouse gas. It builds up in the atmosphere for hundreds of years. Burning fossil fuels releases large amounts of it. The main driver of climate change.
🐄
Methane (CH₄) — very potent
Very potent — about 80 times more powerful than CO₂ over 20 years, but breaks down in about 12 years. Comes from cattle digestion, landfills, and oil and gas leaks.
💧
Water vapour (H₂O) — it's complicated!
Water vapour is actually the most abundant greenhouse gas in our atmosphere, and it is completely natural. Oceans, lakes and rivers naturally put water vapour into the air all the time. However, water vapour cannot build up on its own — it depends on temperature. When CO₂ warms the planet even a little, warmer air holds more water vapour, which traps more heat, which makes the air even warmer, which holds even more vapour. This is called a positive feedback loop — each step makes the next step bigger. CO₂ starts the warming; water vapour amplifies it.
🌾
Nitrous oxide (N₂O)
About 273 times more potent than CO₂ over 100 years. Released from fertilizers used in farming, soils, and burning fuels.
🔵
Nitrogen & Oxygen — NOT greenhouse gases!
These make up 99% of our air but do not trap heat at all. Only gas molecules made of 3 or more atoms can absorb and re-emit infrared radiation.
potent (POH-tent) — very powerful, strong in effect even in small amounts  |  positive feedback loop (POZ-i-tiv FEED-bak loop) — when one change causes a second change that makes the first change even bigger, creating a cycle that keeps growing

Eunice Newton Foote

The woman who discovered the greenhouse effect — and was initially forgotten

📖 A short story

In 1856, Eunice Foote did a simple but brilliant experiment. She filled glass tubes with different gases — ordinary air, damp air, and carbon dioxide — and put them in sunlight. She measured how hot each one got.

Her finding: the tube filled with CO₂ got hotter and stayed hot longer than the others. From this, she worked out that if Earth's ancient atmosphere had contained more CO₂, the planet would have been much warmer. This was the first time anyone showed experimentally what we now call the greenhouse effect.

When her paper was read at a science meeting in 1856, it was read aloud by a male colleague rather than by Foote herself — this was common for women scientists at the time. The Irish scientist John Tyndall is often given credit for discovering CO₂'s warming effect, because he published his own experiments in 1861 — three years after Foote. It is now believed that Foote's work came first, but because she was a woman in the 1800s, her contribution was overlooked for a long time. Today she is recognised as a pioneer, and her name is in the history books.
