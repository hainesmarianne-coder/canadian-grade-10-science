⚛️ Section 01 — Foundation

Electron Shells

How electrons arrange themselves around the nucleus.

🏗️ What is a model?

No one has ever seen an atom directly — they are far too small. So scientists build models: simplified descriptions that explain what we observe in experiments. A model is not a perfect picture of reality. It is the best explanation we have so far.

🔬 How models improve over time
As scientists do new experiments, they gather new evidence. If the evidence fits the current model, the model is kept. If the evidence contradicts it, the model is updated or replaced. This is how science works.

The atomic model has changed several times. Each version was the best explanation available at the time:

🍮 Thomson (1897) — "Plum pudding"

Electrons embedded in a cloud of positive charge — like raisins in a pudding. Proposed after the electron was discovered.

🥇 Rutherford (1911) — Nuclear model

Gold foil experiment showed most of the atom is empty space, with a tiny dense nucleus at the centre. Electrons orbit the outside.

🔵 Bohr (1913) — Shell model

Electrons orbit in fixed shells at specific distances. Each shell holds a set number of electrons. This is the model we use in this course.

📌 Worth remembering
The shell model is a simplification. It is very useful for understanding chemical behaviour, but later scientists discovered that electrons do not actually travel in neat circular paths. The shell model is our best simple tool — not the final word.
🍇 Mind-blowing fact — most of the atom is empty space
Rutherford's experiment showed that atoms are almost entirely empty space, with a tiny, dense nucleus at the centre. If an atom were the size of a football field, the nucleus would be like a grape in the middle, and everything else would be empty. We don’t fall through objects because the electrons on the outside of atoms repel each other, creating the feeling of solidity.

🪣 How Many Electrons Fit?

Each shell has a maximum number of electrons it can hold. Think of each shell like a row of seats — once every seat is filled, the next person must move to the next row.

💡 The big idea
For elements up to and including atomic number 18, electrons always fill the innermost shell first. Only when that shell is full do electrons begin filling the next one. The are more than 4 shells, but that's all we will cover here.

Part 1 The Filling Rule 3 / 15

📥 How to Fill the Shells

Follow these three steps for any element:

  • 1
    Find the atomic number. For a neutral atom, this equals the number of electrons.
  • 2
    Place electrons into Shell 1 first (max 2). Move to Shell 2 when Shell 1 is full, then Shell 3, and so on.
  • 3
    Write the notation — the number of electrons in each shell, separated by commas. Example: 2, 8, 1

✅ Worked Example — Oxygen (O)

Atomic number = 8 → 8 electrons
Shell 1: 2 → 6 remaining
Shell 2: 6
Notation: 2, 6

✅ Worked Example — Sodium (Na)

Atomic number = 11 → 11 electrons
Shell 1: 2 (full) → 9 remaining
Shell 2: 8 (full) → 1 remaining
Shell 3: 1
Notation: 2, 8, 1

✅ Worked Example — Chlorine (Cl)

Atomic number = 17 → 17 electrons
Shell 1: 2 → 15 remaining
Shell 2: 8 → 7 remaining
Shell 3: 7
Notation: 2, 8, 7

Valence Electrons

The electrons in the outermost shell are called valence electrons. They are the only electrons that participate in chemical reactions and bonding. All inner-shell electrons are held tightly and ignored.

📏 Simple rule
The last number in the shell notation = the number of valence electrons.
Na is 2, 8, 1 → 1 valence electron.
Cl is 2, 8, 7 → 7 valence electrons.

Part 2 Ionization 6 / 15
⚡ Section 02 — Ionization

Why Atoms Gain & Lose Electrons

Shell filling explains everything about why atoms become ions.

Atoms are most stable when their outermost shell is completely full. When it is not full, atoms will gain or lose electrons to reach that stable state. This process is called ionization.

🤔 The key question for every atom
Is it easier to lose the few electrons I have in my outer shell — or gain the few electrons I need to fill it?

The answer depends on how many valence electrons the atom has. This is what separates metals from non-metals.

The process by which an atom gains or loses electrons to become an ion.

An atom that has gained or lost electrons and now carries an electric charge.

In chemistry: having a full outer shell. Stable atoms do not need to react.

Part 2 Metals — Losing Electrons 7 / 15

⬆️ Metals Lose Electrons

Metals sit on the left side of the periodic table. They have 1, 2, or 3 valence electrons — just a few electrons sitting alone in their outermost shell.

For a metal, it is far easier to lose those few electrons than to gain 5, 6, or 7 more to fill the shell. Losing them empties the outer shell entirely, making the previous full shell the new outer shell.

🔋 Example — Lithium (Li), Z = 3

Arrangement: 2, 1
Shell 2 has only 1 electron. Needs 7 more to fill — or can lose just 1.
Loses 1 electron → arrangement becomes 2 (Shell 1, full).
Lost a negative electron → now has more protons than electrons → charge is +1.
Becomes the lithium ion: Li⁺

✨ Example — Magnesium (Mg), Z = 12

Arrangement: 2, 8, 2
Shell 3 has only 2 electrons. Loses both.
Loses 2 electrons → arrangement becomes 2, 8 (full).
Charge is +2. Becomes: Mg²⁺

CationKAT-eye-on
A positive ion. Formed when an atom loses electrons. Metals form cations.
MetalMET-ul
An element on the left side of the periodic table. Metals tend to lose electrons.
Li⁺LITH-ee-um EYE-on
The lithium ion. The "+" means it lost one electron and now has a +1 charge.

Part 2 Non-metals — Gaining Electrons 8 / 15

⬇️ Non-metals Gain Electrons

Non-metals sit on the right side of the periodic table. They have 5, 6, or 7 valence electrons — their outer shell is nearly full.

For a non-metal, it is much easier to gain the 1, 2, or 3 electrons needed to fill the outer shell, rather than lose 5, 6, or 7. Gaining electrons completes the outer shell.

🧂 Example — Chlorine (Cl), Z = 17

Arrangement: 2, 8, 7
Shell 3 has 7 electrons — needs just 1 more to be full (max 8).
Gains 1 electron → arrangement becomes 2, 8, 8 (full).
Gained an electron → now has more electrons than protons → charge is −1.
Becomes: Cl⁻

🌬️ Example — Oxygen (O), Z = 8

Arrangement: 2, 6
Shell 2 has 6 electrons — needs 2 more to be full.
Gains 2 electrons → arrangement becomes 2, 8 (full).
Charge is −2. Becomes: O²⁻

💡 Why is oxygen O₂ in the air? 🌬️ Gaining 2 electrons from another atom is one option — but oxygen can also get its 2 missing electrons by sharing with a second oxygen atom. Each oxygen brings 6 valence electrons, they share 2 between them, and both atoms "see" a full shell of 8. This is why oxygen in the air exists as O₂ rather than as single atoms. Sharing electrons is called covalent bonding — something you will study in more detail soon.

Part 2 Metals vs Non-metals 9 / 15

⚖️ Side by Side

Here is the complete picture. Both metals and non-metals are trying to reach the same goal — a full outer shell — but they take opposite paths to get there.

⚙️ Metals — lose electrons

Position: Left side of table
Valence e⁻: 1, 2, or 3
They: Lose electrons
Ion charge: Positive (+)
Ion type: Cation
Examples: Li⁺, Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺

🧪 Non-metals — gain electrons

Position: Right side of table
Valence e⁻: 5, 6, or 7
They: Gain electrons
Ion charge: Negative (−)
Ion type: Anion
Examples: Cl⁻, O²⁻, F⁻, N³⁻

😴 Noble Gases — Group 18

Noble gases (He, Ne, Ar…) already have a full outer shell. They have no need to gain or lose electrons. This is why they are called inert — they almost never react with anything.

⚡ What happens when ions meet?
Ions are highly reactive — opposite charges attract each other strongly. When a cation meets an anion they pull together to form a neutral compound. For example, Na⁺ and Cl⁻ attract each other and form sodium chloride — table salt. Ions with the same charge repel each other and push apart.
⭐ The one rule to remember
Ionization is simply each atom taking the shortest path to a full outer shell.

Part 1 + 2 Periods & Groups 10 / 15

💜 Two Rules to Know by Heart

Everything we have covered — shell filling, valence electrons, ionization — connects directly to the periodic table through exactly two rules.

📋 Rule 1 — Periods
The period number tells you how many shells the atom is using.

Period 1 → 1 shell  |  Period 2 → 2 shells  |  Period 3 → 3 shells …

A new period begins every time electrons start filling a new shell.
📋 Rule 2 — Groups
The group number (for main-group elements: Groups 1, 2, and 13–18) tells you how many valence electrons an atom has. This does not apply to helium (which has 2), or to the transition metals (Groups 3–12).

Group 1 → 1 valence e⁻  |  Group 2 → 2  |  Group 16 → 6  |  Group 17 → 7  |  Group 18 → 8 (full)
ElementNotationPeriodGroup
H11 — uses 1 shell1 — has 1 valence e⁻
Li2, 12 — uses 2 shells1 — has 1 valence e⁻
Mg2, 8, 23 — uses 3 shells2 — has 2 valence e⁻
Cl2, 8, 73 — uses 3 shells17 — has 7 valence e⁻
Ca2, 8, 8, 24 — uses 4 shells2 — has 2 valence e⁻
✏️ What this means in practice
If you are given an element's position on the periodic table — even without its name — you can immediately write its electron arrangement. Period 3, Group 16? That's 3 shells, 6 valence electrons → 2, 8, 6. That's sulfur.

🟢 Practice — Level 1: Recall

🟢 Question 1
What does the atomic number tell us?
The atomic number tells us the number of protons in the nucleus of an atom. Because a neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons, it also tells us the number of electrons. Every element has a unique atomic number — no two elements share the same one.
🟢 Question 2
What would happen to an atom's charge if it were to gain extra electrons? What would happen if it were to lose electrons?
Gaining electrons: The atom gains extra negative charge. It now has more electrons than protons → overall negative charge → becomes an anion (e.g. Cl⁻).

Losing electrons: The atom loses negative charge. It now has more protons than electrons → overall positive charge → becomes a cation (e.g. Na⁺).
🟢 Question 3
If a fluorine atom becomes a fluoride ion, what charge does it have? How did it get that charge?
Fluorine has atomic number 9 → arrangement 2, 7 → 7 valence electrons.
It needs 1 more to fill its outer shell to 8.
Fluorine gains 1 electron → now has 10 electrons but still only 9 protons.
Charge = −1. Written as F⁻ (the fluoride ion).
🟢 Question 4
Explain what the term "atomic model" means.
An atomic model is a simplified description of the structure of an atom. Because atoms are too small to see directly, scientists create models — based on experimental evidence — to explain how atoms behave. Models are not perfect pictures of reality; they are the best explanation available at the time. As new experiments provide new evidence, models are updated or replaced. The current model used in this course is the Bohr shell model.

Practice — Level 2: Definitions & Apply

🟡 Question 5
What is an atomic mass unit (amu)?
An atomic mass unit (amu) is the standard unit used to measure the mass of atoms and subatomic particles. One amu is defined as one twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom. Protons and neutrons each have a mass of approximately 1 amu. Electrons are so light they are often treated as having no mass (their mass is about 1/1836 amu).
🟡 Question 6
How is the mass number different from the atomic mass? What is an isotope? What is a nuclide?
Mass number: A whole number — the total count of protons + neutrons in one specific atom.

Atomic mass: The weighted average mass of all naturally occurring isotopes of an element, expressed in amu. It is usually not a whole number (e.g. chlorine's atomic mass is 35.45 amu).

Isotope: Atoms of the same element (same number of protons) but with different numbers of neutrons, and therefore different mass numbers. E.g. oxygen-16 and oxygen-18.

Nuclide: A specific type of nucleus defined by its number of protons and neutrons. Each isotope is a different nuclide.
🟡 Question 7
For oxygen-16 and oxygen-18, state the number of protons, neutrons, and electrons in each.
Both are oxygen → atomic number = 8 → 8 protons, 8 electrons (neutral).

Oxygen-16: mass number = 16 → neutrons = 16 − 8 = 8 neutrons
Protons: 8  |  Neutrons: 8  |  Electrons: 8

Oxygen-18: mass number = 18 → neutrons = 18 − 8 = 10 neutrons
Protons: 8  |  Neutrons: 10  |  Electrons: 8

These are isotopes — same element, same electrons, different neutron count.
🟡 Question 8
Oxygen has three naturally occurring isotopes: oxygen-16, oxygen-17, and oxygen-18. Their abundances are approximately 99.76%, 0.04%, and 0.20% respectively.

a) What does it mean that these are all "oxygen" despite having different mass numbers?
b) The atomic mass of oxygen on the periodic table is 15.999 amu. Why is it so close to 16 rather than being an average of 16, 17, and 18?
c) Could two atoms of oxygen-16 and oxygen-18 be considered the same element? Explain.
a) They are all oxygen because they all have 8 protons. The atomic number — not the mass number — defines the element. They differ only in their number of neutrons (8, 9, and 10 respectively).

b) The atomic mass is a weighted average based on abundance. Because oxygen-16 makes up nearly 99.76% of all oxygen atoms, it dominates the average and pulls it very close to 16. The tiny amounts of oxygen-17 and oxygen-18 barely shift the result.

c) Yes — they are the same element. Same number of protons (8), same number of electrons (8), same chemical behaviour. They are simply different isotopes of oxygen. Chemically they are identical; they only differ in mass.
🟡 Question 9
a) How many protons are in a hydrogen nucleus?
b) How many neutrons are in hydrogen-1?
c) How many electrons does a hydrogen atom have?
d) How does a hydrogen cation differ from a hydrogen atom? What is another name for it?
a) Hydrogen has atomic number 1 → 1 proton
b) Hydrogen-1 has mass number 1, so neutrons = 1 − 1 = 0 neutrons
c) Neutral hydrogen has 1 electron
d) A hydrogen cation (H⁺) has lost its 1 electron → it is just a proton

🟠 Question 10
How does the iron(III) ion differ from the iron(II) ion?
Both are iron atoms (atomic number = 26) that have lost electrons to form positive ions.

Iron(II) — Fe²⁺: has lost 2 electrons → charge of +2
Iron(III) — Fe³⁺: has lost 3 electrons → charge of +3

Fe³⁺ has one fewer electron than Fe²⁺. Both are cations, but Fe³⁺ is more positively charged.
🟠 Question 11
Use the periodic table to put the following elements into pairs that would be expected to have similar chemical properties:

Ba, S, Br, Ca, K, F, Se, Na
Elements in the same group have the same number of valence electrons and behave similarly:

Ba & Ca — both Group 2 (2 valence electrons, both lose 2 to form 2+ ions)
K & Na — both Group 1 (1 valence electron, both lose 1 to form 1+ ions)
S & Se — both Group 16 (6 valence electrons, both gain 2 to form 2− ions)
Br & F — both Group 17 (7 valence electrons, both gain 1 to form 1− ions)

🔴 Question 15
The three subatomic particles each have very different masses.

a) Which subatomic particle is the lightest?
b) A proton has a mass of 1 amu. How does the mass of a neutron compare?
c) An electron has a mass of 0.00055 amu. How many times heavier is a proton than an electron?
d) What does this tell us about where most of the mass of an atom is found?
a) The electron is by far the lightest — much lighter than either the proton or neutron.

b) A neutron has almost exactly the same mass as a proton — approximately 1 amu. They are nearly identical in mass.

c) 1 ÷ 0.00055 ≈ 1836. A proton is about 1836 times heavier than an electron.

d) Because protons and neutrons are ~1836 times heavier than electrons, almost all of an atom's mass is concentrated in the nucleus. The electrons orbiting outside contribute almost nothing to the total mass.
🔴 Question 16
All matter is composed of atoms, which are in turn composed mostly of empty space. Why is it not possible to walk through a wall or to put your hand through your desk?
Even though atoms are mostly empty space, the electrons in the outermost shells of atoms carry a negative charge. When two objects are pressed together, the electrons on the surface atoms of each object repel each other strongly (like charges repel). This repulsion feels like a solid barrier — it is the electromagnetic force between electrons, not physical "solidity," that stops your hand passing through the desk.

In short: matter feels solid because of electrical repulsion between electrons, not because there is no space inside atoms.

📚 End of Class Summary

What we covered

Atoms have a tiny nucleus of protons and neutrons with electrons arranged in shells. Electrons fill innermost shells first, and the outermost valence electrons determine how atoms behave and form ions.

Key takeaways

  • Shell capacities are 2, 8, 8 for the first three shells.
  • Atomic number equals the number of electrons in a neutral atom.
  • Metals usually lose electrons to form cations; non-metals often gain electrons to form anions.
  • Noble gases have full outer shells and are mostly unreactive.
